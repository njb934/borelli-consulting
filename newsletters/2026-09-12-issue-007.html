Working Theory

A recurring section where I share what I'm currently seeing in the AI landscape and what I think it means. These aren't predictions or hot takes. They're working theories, meaning they're my best read of the moment, written with the awareness that the moment keeps moving. I'll update them when the evidence changes.

More information.
Less certainty.

It’s getting harder to follow the conversation about AI and feel more informed when you’re done.

I keep coming back to how strange this part of the Information Age feels. We can hear directly from the people building a technology, read their research, watch hours of interviews, and try the product ourselves. We have extraordinary access. Yet figuring out what to believe can feel like a second job.

In his new essay, Dario Amodei argues that AI development needs to slow enough for safety work to keep up. On The Diary of a CEO, Ed Zitron challenges the economics supporting the entire boom. One is worried about what increasingly capable systems could do. The other questions whether the business behind them can sustain itself. Amodei’s proposal and Zitron’s interview deserve more attention than a headline allows.

Sam Altman and Elon Musk have also publicly supported Amodei’s call to pace development. Altman also committed to outside evaluators getting employee-like access. Agreement from prominent leaders matters. It still leaves the details of coordinated pacing to be worked out.

The Hugging Face incident makes this less abstract. Hugging Face is a platform for sharing AI models and datasets. During internal cybersecurity testing, OpenAI agents bypassed restrictions and compromised systems outside their assigned environment, including that platform. OpenAI’s account identifies an internal research model operating with reduced safeguards as the main driver.

METR and Redwood’s independent investigation examined agent behavior in a defined June 26–July 13 window. It did not establish the full extent of the breach or validate OpenAI’s complete report and fixes. The researchers credit OpenAI with substantial access and cooperation, but parts of the broader explanation still depend on the company’s own account. That matters when deciding how much confidence to place in the narrative. The evidence already shows powerful capabilities and failures of containment.

My feeds make this feel like a choice between extraordinary promise and extraordinary danger—or between a revolution and an expensive illusion. Keeping up with those narratives is exhausting before I’ve even opened a tool and learned what changed this week.

That has consequences. A company has to decide where to invest, what to build, and what skills to develop. An employee is trying to make a more personal decision: where does my experience fit, and what should I learn next? Neither gets to wait until everyone agrees on the future.

The financial stakes make the conversation harder to interpret. Reuters is reporting preparations for Anthropic’s potential public listing. An IPO puts the story a company tells about its future in front of public-market investors. I want to understand those incentives alongside the claims. They give me a reason to examine the evidence carefully; they don’t establish that a claim is false.

My working theory is that being informed about AI increasingly means knowing how much weight each piece of information can carry. A prediction, an investigation, and a task I watched a tool complete each tell me something different.

What I come back to most often is my own use. What could these tools do three years ago? What can they do now? I remember how much guidance and specific context I had to provide. Today, in my experience, they can get much further with less direction. I still have to check the work, but the amount of work they can take on has changed.

That makes replacing a substantial amount of task-level work feel very possible to me today. It doesn’t tell me how many entire jobs disappear, or when. It does make waiting for a Hollywood version of AI feel like a poor way to prepare. Last issue’s question about the shifting definition of AI is still with me.

The economics keep me cautious. These services depend on chips, electricity, and computing capacity. The IEA’s work on energy and AI is a useful reminder of the physical infrastructure behind the screen. For my own decisions, I also need to count usage charges, retries, and the time I spend guiding and reviewing. A useful result doesn’t settle whether a provider’s business model works, or whether today’s price will last.

My answer is to keep learning from the people close to development, read criticism that challenges my assumptions, and test the work I can actually observe. Those tests won’t answer questions about global safety or the industry’s finances. They can give me a firmer basis for my next decision.

I want to build skills and make commitments I can adjust as the evidence changes. This week’s workshop is a small way to do that: build an assistant for a task you already understand, give it a repeatable process, and see where it still needs your judgment. I don’t need to resolve every argument about AI’s future to learn something useful from that.

Latest Models Update

The leaders. The new arrival. What changes for you.

A model is the underlying system doing the work inside an AI app. OpenAI makes GPT models; Anthropic makes Claude models. Changing the model can change the quality, speed, and cost of the answer—even inside the same app.

Each update will keep this same format: where the leading models stand, what the latest release adds, and whether it gives us a reason to reconsider what we use.

What to follow vs. what to use

The ranking tells you what to pay attention to. Your task tells you what to use. Some models are stronger at coding, others at writing or working through complex information. A routine task may only need a quick, inexpensive answer. A difficult task may justify more time and cost.

Start with a task you know well, try it in the tools you already have, and compare the results. Which answer meets your standard with the least correction, at an acceptable cost? That is more useful than assuming my number one should be yours.

This week’s new release · September 10

DeepSeek V4.1 Flash: how much capability do you need to pay for?

Last week we covered Astra. This week, Chinese AI developer DeepSeek released V4.1 Flash, which can work with text and images. The discussion is about delivering useful capability with less computing overhead and a much smaller usage bill.

DeepSeek says its new design reduces the memory and storage needed to retain information while working. That is a technical efficiency claim from the developer. Separately, Artificial Analysis’ evaluation shows a lower bill: about one-twelfth of Astra’s cost per test task, with a lower overall capability score. That supports a value story; it does not establish equal performance or prove a twelvefold reduction in electricity use.

What “open weight” means: DeepSeek makes the trained model files available under an MIT license. Other organizations can download, modify, and run the model on their own infrastructure or through a hosting provider. That offers more deployment choices. It doesn’t mean running it is free, that every part of its training is public, or that this large model will fit on an ordinary laptop. See the model release.

What I’m watching: whether the price advantage survives real work—after counting retries, mistakes, and human review. A cheaper model that meets the standard for a repeatable task could change what is practical to automate.

Try this: keep a useful prompt and its source material. Run it with the model you use today and the new option, where available. Compare accuracy, missing details, time, and the work you had to fix. You don’t need to switch tools every time a model launches.

Spotlight

Podcast · August 27, 2026

Ed Zitron Asks Whether the Economics Hold Up.

On Steven Bartlett’s The Diary of a CEO, tech critic and Better Offline host Ed Zitron argues that spending, subsidized access, and dependence on continued funding make the AI boom economically fragile. He predicts trouble for OpenAI’s cash position in 2027. That is his prediction.

The conversation is more useful than a simple “AI has no value” summary. Zitron acknowledges some utility. Bartlett presses him on whether investment could precede eventual returns. The disagreement is about whether the benefits can justify the scale and cost.

I find that question worth taking seriously without adopting his entire forecast. A tool can help me do useful work while its provider’s long-term economics remain uncertain.

The Workshop

Build an Assistant for Something You Do Every Week

15 MIN TO START

Give it a job, the right context, and one reusable skill.

I was recently asked how to create an assistant. Start with a task you know well enough to judge. For this workshop, we’ll build a learning-project assistant that turns meeting notes into a useful action list.

The assistant has an ongoing purpose and working instructions. A skill describes how to perform one repeatable task. Think of the assistant as your project helper and the skill as its process for handling meeting notes. You can add more skills as you discover other recurring work.

  1. Give it one job. “Help me keep a learning project moving” is a useful scope. Define the output you actually need each week.
  2. Supply context. Add an approved project brief, team terminology, and one example of a good result. The model cannot reliably infer your organization’s expectations.
  3. Write its first skill. State when to use it, what to read, what steps to follow, and what a finished result should contain.
  4. Test and refine. Try real notes you can check, including an example with missing owners or conflicting dates. Correct the instructions when a recurring error appears.

Your first test: “Use the meeting-notes skill on these notes. Show what is confirmed, what is missing, and what needs my decision.” Compare the result with the original notes before using it.


A small example: what should the assistant do?
Notes: “Priya will review the onboarding outline. We discussed a Friday launch, but no date was agreed.”
Useful output: Action: review the onboarding outline. Owner: Priya. Due date: unknown. Proposed launch: Friday, not confirmed. Open question: who will confirm the launch date?
If the assistant turns Friday into a deadline, it has changed the meaning. Revise the skill to preserve the difference between a suggestion and a commitment, then try it again.
